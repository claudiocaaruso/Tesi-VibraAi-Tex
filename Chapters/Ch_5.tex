\section{Exploratory PCA of the dataset}
\label{sec:pca_dataset}

In this work, PCA was first used as an exploratory tool to inspect the structure of the Raman dataset before building the classification model. To perform this step, PCA was calculated on the entire dataset after removing the non-informative labels, namely unlabelled/transparent pixels and artefacts, as described in \Cref{sec:labels}.

As discussed previously, PCA is an unsupervised machine learning method. The final objective of this analysis is therefore not to decide which spectral components are more relevant to distinguish different biological classes within samples or to perform classification, but rather to investigate the content of the spectra in terms of explained variance to understand whether they contain particularly relevant directions and whether a more compact representation may be adequate for our dataset. If this were the case, it would be possible to reduce the dimensionality of our data to the number of components most suitable for training the artificial neural network, for the reasons explained in \Cref{sec:dimensionality_reduction}. 


The data was oranized in matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) introduced in
\Cref{sec:dimensionality_reduction}.
The matrix was constructed so that each column corresponds to one of the 483 bands and each row contains the intensities.

\Cref{fig:original_centered_spectrum} visually represents the centring of one single spectrum as described in \Cref{subsec:pca_centering_covariance}. This step is applied to all spectra in the dataset, and the new principal directions are calculated on the centered matrix \(\mathbf{X}_c\).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{Images/images_ch_5/05_original_vs_centered_spectrum.png}
    \caption{Example of an original Raman spectrum and the corresponding centered spectrum used for PCA.}
    \label{fig:original_centered_spectrum}
\end{figure}


After computing the new principal components, the most relevant parameter evaluated is the \textit{Explained Variance Ratio} (EVR) of each component, explained in \Cref{subsec:pca_svd}. The scree plot in 
\Cref{fig:pca_scree_plot} shows how most of the variance is retained by the first components, consistent with the theory, as we expect the new set of directions to be ordered, while the contribution of later components rapidly becomes smaller and negligible.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{Images/images_ch_5/07_scree_plot_explained_variance.png}
    \caption{Explained variance associated with the first principal components.}
    \label{fig:pca_scree_plot}
\end{figure}

The \textit{Cumulative Explained Variance} (CEV) plotted in \Cref{fig:pca_cumulative_variance} shows a steep initial increase and a progressive flattening of the curve. This proves how the last components are significantly less relevant considering the amount of information they contain; more specifically, we observe that keeping a representation made by the first 89 components only allows us to reach the 95 per cent explained variance threshold.

\begin{figure}[H]

    \caption{Cumulative explained variance as a function of the number of retained principal components.}
    \label{fig:pca_cumulative_variance}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:pca_reconstruction_comparison} shows the effect of reconstructing the same
spectrum with just one component and with \(89\) components. The first component is able to capture the dominant trend, whereas the reconstruction using \(89\) components preserves the main spectral profile while providing a more compact representation.

    }
    \caption{Comparison between an original Raman spectrum and its PCA reconstruction using different numbers of retained principal components.}
    \label{fig:pca_reconstruction_comparison}
\end{figure}


In the PCA space, each spectrum can be visualised as a point in a 483-dimensional space if all principal directions are considered, and the Raman dataset is therefore a point cloud.  Reducing the representation to fewer components also reduces the dimensionality of this space, projecting the data onto the directions along which the data cloud extends the most (a new subspace), which are equivalent to the first principal directions.

The score plots in \Cref{fig:pca_score_pc1_pc2} and \Cref{fig:pca_score_pc1_pc483} were generated on a random subset of
\(10000\) spectra, for better readability.

\Cref{fig:pca_score_pc1_pc2} shows how the cloud is distributed along both the PC1 and PC2 axes, with a wider spatial extent along the former, consistent with the fact that the former component explains a greater variance. Points associated with spectra labelled differently, depicted in different colours, overlap, indicating that the biological classes are not distinguishable in this examination. More specifically, it is relevant to point attention to the distribution of the tumoral class previously defined: label 2 (\textit{tumor}) and label 20 (\textit{tumor with necrosis}) seem to be concentrated more on the right side of the cloud, suggesting a possible pattern of distinction from other labels. Nevertheless, this does not apply consistently to all the points represented. Many appear widely distributed, suggesting strong spectral similarity to several of the tissues under consideration and do not allow for definitive conclusions. PCA is unsupervised, so, as noticed before, it is not necessarily expected to find a criterion for classification; supervised machine learning methods will be required.
 
\Cref{fig:pca_score_pc1_pc483} highlights the large difference in the distribution of data along the first and last components calculated by PCA. The cloud lies along the first principal component and is compressed almost to zero along PC483, with a few exceptions with respect to the number of data points.

\section{Hyperparameter selection}
\label{sec:hyperparameter_selection}

%check if the title is good or it must be changed

The configuration adopted for the final classification experiments was selected through the hyperparameter search described in \Cref{sec:training}, which compared different model sizes, numbers of retained PCA components and batch sizes.

The comparison of the tested configurations showed comparable results for all three architectures (small (S), medium (M), and large (L)) at an equal number of retained PCs. A substantial improvement in the metrics was registered when raising the number of PCs from 1 to 20 (71\% of explained variance) for the macro-class problem (\textit{tumor} vs. \textit{healthy}), and a plateau from 20 to 483 PCs (\cref{fig:heatmap_auc_macroclass}). In the single-class problem (\textit{tumor} vs. \textit{tumor stroma}), the metrics improved from 1 to 10 (55\% of explained variance), flattening out for bigger dimensionalities (\cref{fig:heatmap_auc_singleclass}).

\end{figure}

    \label{fig:heatmap_auc_singleclass}

Although some configurations with more components or larger networks obtained equally good scores for AUC, their training curves showed less stable behavior and a larger discrepancy between training and validation performance that also increased with the number of epochs. This behavior, exemplified by \cref{fig:overfitting_example_L200}, is a clear sign of overfitting. The small model, on the contrary, showed more similarity between training and validation for the considered metrics (\cref{fig:training_curves}) and was therefore preferred as a final choice, matching the metrics of larger architectures at a lower computational cost.

The growing separation between training and validation curves is a sign of overfitting.}
    \label{fig:overfitting_example_L200}
\end{figure}

The maximum number of epochs was set to \(100\), although \textit{early stopping} (\cref{sec:training}) often halted the training as early as epoch 20 due to validation already plateauing, with some folds training for up to 80 epochs before stopping (\cref{fig:training_curves}). \textit{Early stopping} often acted prematurely due to noise in the validation curves when working with batch sizes smaller than 2048. Increasing batch size in fact averages the gradient descent step over more observations, smoothing out the curve and speeding up training, while also raising the risk of converging towards sharp minima, which would lead to poor generalization. The final value for the batch size was set to \(4096\), found to be a good compromise for this task.

%talk about computational complexity 

The decision threshold used to convert the predicted probability into a binary label was selected empirically by comparing threshold values between \(0{,}45\) and \(0{,}50\). The final value was set to \(\tau=0{,}49\), slightly below the standard threshold of \(0{,}50\), as it provided a compromise between favoring recall of the positive class and maintaining balanced overall performance. In this application, a false negative, corresponding to a tumor-related spectrum classified as non-tumor, is considered more critical than a false positive, corresponding to a non-tumor spectrum classified as tumor-related. A slightly lower threshold increases the tendency of the classifier to assign spectra to the positive class, supporting recall for tumor-related spectra without excessively penalizing accuracy, as seen in \cref{sec:evaluation_metrics}.

\section{Aggregated classification results}
\label{sec:aggregated_classification_results}

The configurations chosen for the two approaches, listed in \cref{tab:selected_hyperparameters}, were further analyzed to assess their effectiveness.

The confusion matrices and ROC curves, explained in \cref{sec:evaluation_metrics}, showed good results for both problems. The macro-class approach performed slightly worse than the single-class one, especially in non-tumoral classification, with true negative rates equaled 0,71 and 0,75 respectively, while tumoral recall achieved 0,74 in the first problem and 0,76 for the second. The imbalance in TNR is a direct consequence of the internal heterogeneity of the healthy class. The ROC curves showed symmetrical behavior with respect to the TPR and FNR, with high variability depending on the validation fold composition. Mean AUC across 5 folds achieved scores of $\text{AUC} = 0{,}7931\pm0{,}0390$, relative standard deviation $\sigma_{relative} = 4{,}9\%$, for the macro-class problem (\cref{fig:results_S20_macroclass}), and $\text{AUC} = 0{,}836\pm0{,}043$, $\sigma_{relative} = 5{,}1\%$, for the single-class problem (\cref{fig:results_S10_singleclass}). The difference in mean AUC once again indicates that, on average, the macro-class approach is more difficult than the single-class one.

\section{Per-sample analysis}
\label{sec:per_sample_analysis}

For a more critical analysis, it is important to understand whether errors are evenly distributed across the dataset or are concentrated in specific samples. Despite the large number of spectra in the dataset, they are derived from only \(25\) patients, so many of the spectra do not count as independent observations because they may exhibit similarities. In our specific case, it is crucial to carry out this type of investigation instead of assessing performance across all pixels together, whose overall average may not be sufficiently representative of the model's real capabilities. The per-sample analysis highlights the internal inconsistency of the test and the model's ability to generalise across different patients and specimens. 


\Cref{fig:sample_f1_comparison} shows the \textit{F1-score} calculated by partitioning the test set pixels by sample and comparing the model's performance for each group.
F1 was chosen because it combines \textit{precision} and \textit{recall}, evaluating the quality of the prediction on the positive class: a high \textit{F1} means that the model recognises the \textit{tumour} class well within the same sample. Conversely, a low value indicates that a specific sample is difficult, with many \textit{false positives}, \textit{false negatives}, or both.
Although the AUC is informative for the global evaluation, it was not selected to carry out this comparison as it requires both classes to be present within each evaluated group; \textit{F1} instead can be used more consistently across samples with different class content.

First of all, the result of this comparison confirms what has already been observed in \Cref{sec:aggregated_classification_results}, namely that the macro-class problem is more complicated and unstable because it introduces greater intra-class heterogeneity, with an average \textit{F1} per sample of \(0{,}713\), compared with \(0{,}792\) for the single-class task.
Some of the most difficult classified samples in the \textit{tumour} vs \textit{healthy} experiment improve in the \textit{tumour} vs \textit{stroma} one; the most obvious case is \(3535\_019\) which goes from \textit{F1} approximately \(0{,}43\) to about \(0{,}77\) between the two experiments. This supports the thesis that the difficulty in predictions is closely linked to the definition of the negative class.

Other cases, instead, show poor performance in both experiments, as in \(3439\_009\). The sample-specific difficulty could be related to spectral differences typical of an individual patient or specimen. In these cases, the model fails to generalise adequately due to the high biological variance found.
Furthermore, it is not excluded that a very poor performance on a sample may depend on the quality of the annotations.

More specifically, a result of particular merit is the one of the \(7 \times 7\) filter. This leads to clearly better predictions than smaller filters, in terms of global metrics.
Larger kernels tend to increase \textit{tumour} \textit{recall}, which could be desirable based on what was discussed earlier, but at the same time they lead to an overly aggressive imbalance in the confusion matrix by increasing \textit{false positives} and thus reducing TNR and the overall reliability of the classifier. This is why, although the 10x10 filter shows a higher \textit{AUC}, it is not the best compromise when evaluated against the \textit{TNR} and would represent an unwise choice.

The results of applying a filter as large as \(15 \times 15\) make clear that the benefits of smoothing are limited, and that excessively increasing the filter size leads to deterioration, "spreading" the \textit{tumour} class onto the healthy one.
